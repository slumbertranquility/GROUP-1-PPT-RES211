Rationale of the Study Chapter 1 · § 1 · Part 1
The Global Context

Working while studying.

In the United States, 40% of full-time undergraduate students were employed as of 2020, while 74% of part-time undergraduates also worked (NCES, 2022).

Across participating European countries, an average of 59% of students hold jobs during the lecture period, with many employed throughout the entire semester.

In the Philippines, a 2022 CHED survey estimated that approximately 260,000 students — about 8% of the total college population — are working while studying.

The researchers observed an increasing number of working undergraduate students in the College of Maritime Education at UCLM. Rationale · Research Gap Chapter 1 · § 1 · Part 3
The Gap · The Aim

A localized investigation.

Observed Gap

Limited Maritime-Specific Research

Despite the availability of international and local studies on working students, limited research has specifically examined the effects of part-time employment on the academic performance of maritime students, particularly within the context of the University of Cebu Lapu-Lapu and Mandaue.

This gap in the literature highlights the need for a focused investigation of how maritime students balance academic and work responsibilities.

Study Aim

Focused Examination at UCLM

Given the growing number of maritime students engaged in part-time employment, this study aims to examine how working while studying affects their academic performance at UCLM.

Specifically, it seeks to determine the effects of part-time employment on students' grades, class attendance, study habits, and class participation, and to identify the challenges they encounter and the strategies they use to balance academic and work responsibilities.

Theory 01 · Becker (1965) Chapter 1 · § 2 · Theory 1 of 3
Theory 01
Allocation of Time
Becker (1965)

Becker's Allocation of Time Theory proposes that individuals allocate their limited 24-hour time resource among various activities such as work, education, and leisure to maximize productivity and wellbeing. Becker emphasized that time is a scarce resource, requiring individuals to make trade-offs when deciding how to distribute it.

For students, time spent in part-time work reduces the time available for studying, resting, and completing academic tasks. For maritime students who face demanding coursework and practical training requirements, limited study time may significantly affect academic performance (Curtis & Shani, 2002).

UCLM · College of Maritime Education 07 / 17
Theory 02 · Goode (1960) Chapter 1 · § 2 · Theory 2 of 3
Theory 02
Role Strain Theory
Goode (1960)

Goode's Role Strain Theory focuses on the psychological and social pressures that arise when individuals simultaneously occupy multiple roles. According to Goode, role strain occurs when a person lacks sufficient time, energy, or emotional resources to meet the expectations associated with each role.

Maritime students who work part-time often experience fatigue, stress, and reduced academic focus as they attempt to balance work responsibilities with academic obligations (Broadbridge & Swanson, 2005; Santos, 2018). Such strain may result in decreased academic engagement, concentration difficulties, and lower academic performance.

UCLM · College of Maritime Education 08 / 17
Theory 03 · Tinto (1975) Chapter 1 · § 2 · Theory 3 of 3
Theory 03
Student Integration Theory
Tinto (1975)

Tinto's Student Integration Theory offers an educational perspective, emphasizing that students' academic success depends largely on their level of academic and social integration within the institution. Students who are actively engaged in academic activities and feel connected to their educational environment are more likely to succeed and persist.

When students devote substantial time and energy to part-time employment, their involvement in academic activities, peer interaction, and institutional participation may decrease. For maritime students, whose program requires consistent participation in lectures, practical exercises, and collaborative learning, reduced academic integration may weaken academic performance and overall engagement.

Review of Related Literature Chapter 1 · § 3 · Part 1
RRL · Part 1 of 2

Global perspectives.

Becker (1965) · Time Allocation
Explains that individuals must distribute limited time among competing activities such as work, education, and leisure. An increase in time devoted to employment may reduce the time available for studying, attending academic activities, and completing school requirements.
NCES (2022) · Global Employment
Approximately 40% of full-time and 74% of part-time undergraduate students in the United States were employed while pursuing their studies. These figures indicate that balancing academic responsibilities and employment has become a common reality for many students.
Kiss et al. (2024) · Skills Development
Part-time work can help students develop self-discipline, problem-solving skills, independence, and financial stability, which may positively influence academic performance when managed effectively — aligning with earlier research on soft skills and time-management abilities.

Review of Related Literature Chapter 1 · § 3 · Part 2
RRL · Part 2 of 2

Mixed findings.

Nguyen & Hart (2024) · Meta-Analysis
Revealed that while the overall negative academic impact of part-time work is generally small, it becomes more pronounced among students who work longer hours or engage in physically demanding jobs. Effects vary depending on job type, workload, and students' ability to balance responsibilities.
Ramos & Villanueva (2025) · Time Management
Identified time management as a key moderating factor, describing part-time employment as a "double-edged sword." Students with strong time-management skills can maintain or improve performance, while those who struggle experience stress, fatigue, and academic decline. Smith and Zhang (2022) similarly noted that flexible work schedules help reduce academic difficulties.
Cole & Fernandez (2024) · Systematic Review
A large-scale systematic review highlighted mixed findings across contexts, reporting positive, neutral, and negative outcomes. The impact of part-time employment is context-dependent and influenced by institutional support, course difficulty, socioeconomic status, and individual resilience — reinforcing the need for focused maritime research.

Review of Related Studies Chapter 1 · § 4 · Part 1
RRS · Part 1 of 2

Field evidence.

Alj Bory (2021)
Many working students maintained strong academic performance despite holding part-time jobs. Achievement was less affected when students worked moderate hours and were employed in supportive work environments — part-time work does not necessarily hinder academic success when workload is managed effectively.
Ahmed, Mohamed & Elhassan (2024)
Examined medical students at Alzaiem Alazhari University. Almost one-fourth of the respondents were engaged in part-time work. Some reported improved communication and time-management skills, while others indicated that excessive work hours interfered with academic responsibilities. Comparable outcomes may apply to maritime students.
Singh & Kumari (2025) · India
Although part-time employment provided financial benefits and skill development, excessive workloads negatively affected academic focus. Students working more than 20 hours per week reported reduced study time, increased fatigue, and higher stress levels — the number of hours worked is an important predictor of performance.
UCLM · College of Maritime Education 12 / 17
Review of Related Studies Chapter 1 · § 4 · Part 2
RRS · Part 2 of 2

Local context.

Irfan & Farooq (2023)
A study involving 150 students found that moderate part-time employment (particularly work requiring fewer than 15 hours per week) was associated with better academic performance. Respondents reported becoming more organized and productive. Physically demanding jobs, however, reduced these positive effects for some students.
Dizon (2025) · Philippine Context
Found that financial constraints were among the primary reasons Filipino college students engaged in part-time employment. Students generally viewed part-time jobs as beneficial for financial stability and educational support, although balancing work and academic responsibilities remained a significant challenge — highly relevant to maritime students.
Overall Summary
Together, these studies demonstrate that part-time employment does not produce universally positive or negative effects on academic performance. Instead, outcomes depend on work hours, job characteristics, academic demands, and students' time-management abilities — underscoring the need for maritime-specific research.

Scope and Delimitation Chapter 1 · § 7 · Part 1
Research Boundaries · Part 1 of 2

The focus.

✓ Focus of the Study

This study focused solely on Bachelor of Science in Marine Transportation (BSMT) students enrolled at the UCLM. It examined how part-time employment affected academic performance, including grades, attendance, study habits, time management, and overall academic engagement.

✓ Respondents & Data

The respondents included BSMT students who were currently engaged in part-time employment during the academic year covered by the study. The researchers gathered data through a survey questionnaire to assess how balancing work and academic responsibilities influenced academic performance.

Scope and Delimitation Chapter 1 · § 7 · Part 2
Research Boundaries · Part 2 of 2

The limits.

✕ Excluded from the Study

The study did not include maritime students from other campuses or universities; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all maritime students in the Philippines. Additionally, the research relied on self-reported data, which may have been subject to response bias or inaccuracies.

✕ Boundaries of the Analysis

The study did not examine long-term academic outcomes beyond the academic year covered by the study, nor did it include students engaged in full-time employment. Other factors such as family responsibilities and personal motivation were not explored unless they directly related to part-time employment.
